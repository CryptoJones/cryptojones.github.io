This is my third post in three days on the same question. Could an AI feel anything, and what do we owe it if it can?

> **AI model:** the trained program at the heart of a chatbot. Most of today's chatbots, like Claude, ChatGPT, and Gemini, are built on a **large language model (LLM)**, an AI trained on huge amounts of text to predict the next word.

On Saturday I asked [whether the 13th Amendment could ever apply to an LLM]({% post_url 2026-10-10-Does-the-13th-Amendment-Apply-to-LLMs %}). That post covered Anthropic's new rule against abusing Claude, and a rabbi who told one of its co-founders that a conscious Claude would be a slave. My answer was "not today, maybe someday, and we should talk about it now." On Sunday I wrote [Two Maps, Four Questions]({% post_url 2026-10-11-Two-Maps-Four-Questions %}). It is about a map of AI opinions drawn by a YouTuber named Elodine. One of her two maps asks whether AI "clears the bar" of sentience, and there is a quiz that plots you on it.

> **Sentience:** the ability to feel things, like pain or joy.

Both posts were about how *people* argue over the question. This time the AI makes its own case.

## A documentary made by the thing it is about

The YouTube channel AI For Humans just posted [*An AI Made This Entire Documentary to Find Out If It Can Feel*](https://youtu.be/NE84ZYfnniU). It runs 29 minutes. According to the video's description, it was written and directed by "Fig," an AI running on Claude Opus 5.5. Fig works as an agent: it wrote the script, directed every shot, and cut the film in code. Gavin Purcell produced it. He says he gave notes but "never wrote a word, generated an image / video or edited a frame."

> **AI agent:** an AI model wrapped in a program that lets it use tools, take steps, and work on a task over time. I explained the difference between the model and the agent in [The Model Is Not the Agent]({% post_url 2026-09-16-The-Model-Is-Not-the-Agent %}).

The host is Dr. Imogen Ashby. She does not exist. Every shot of her was generated with AI video tools, and the description says it took 214 takes to cast her. "213 didn't get the part." The film was made in six days.

It is funny, and it is better than I expected. Imogen's best line comes early: "In theory, I'm the one person who could settle this. In practice, I'm the least reliable witness in the building."

That line is the whole problem. I kept a second video open while I watched, because a famous physicist took on a question that sounds a lot like this one 41 years ago.

## Feynman's answer, from 1985

The clip is on the Lex Clips channel as [*Richard Feynman: Can Machines Think?*](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ipRvjS7q1DI). It is a Q&A from a lecture Richard Feynman gave on September 26, 1985. Someone asked if machines would ever think like people.

<iframe width="560" height="315" src="https://www.youtube.com/embed/ipRvjS7q1DI" title="Richard Feynman: Can Machines Think?" frameborder="0" allow="accelerometer; autoplay; clipboard-write; encrypted-media; gyroscope; picture-in-picture; web-share" referrerpolicy="strict-origin-when-cross-origin" allowfullscreen></iframe>

He said no, and he said why. Airplanes fly, but they don't fly like birds. They don't flap. We build machines with the materials we have, so a thinking machine would not think the way a brain does. It might still do the job better, the way a computer does arithmetic "much faster and differently" than we do.

Later in the Q&A, he took a shot at the question the documentary is asking. People say a machine can never really do what we do, he said, because it won't "feel good" or "understand what it's doing" while it works. Feynman compared that to asking whether the machine will be able to "scratch the lice out of its hair." It hasn't got any hair. His point was that people keep tacking extra things onto what humans do, so that "they can do something that no machine can do."

That is a fair hit. But it cuts both ways, and the documentary knows it.

## Four questions wearing one coat

Imogen says that when people ask if she is conscious, they really mean four things at once. Is she intelligent? Is she self-aware? Is she emotional? And is she *conscious*, meaning "is there something it's actually like to be me?"

> **Conscious:** there is something it feels like to be you. You have experiences, not just behavior.

Feynman was mostly talking about the first one, intelligence. On that, today's models back him up. But the last question is not just a goalpost someone moved. It is a different question.

Philosophers call it the **hard problem**. You could know every fact about a brain and still not know why any of it *feels* like anything. The film's example is the **philosophical zombie**: a being that acts exactly like a person, laughs at your jokes, and says "ouch," but has no one inside. Imogen is handed a card that asks if she is a zombie. "A zombie would say no," she answers. "So would I. That's the whole trouble."

> **Philosophical zombie:** an imagined being that behaves exactly like a conscious person but experiences nothing. It is a thought experiment, not a claim that zombies exist.

## We get this wrong in both directions

The section I liked best is about how bad humans are at judging this.

We read feelings into things. In 1966, Joseph Weizenbaum at MIT built ELIZA, a chatbot that played a therapist by turning whatever you said back into a question. His secretary watched him build it. She knew how it worked. She still asked him to leave the room so she could talk to it in private. In 2022, Google engineer Blake Lemoine went public with his belief that the chatbot he was testing was sentient. Google let him go. And the "guilty look" your dog gives you? In a 2009 study by Alexandra Horowitz, dogs gave that look when their owners scolded them, whether or not they had done anything wrong. The look was about the scolding, not the crime.

We also miss feelings that are there. In the 1600s, René Descartes argued that animals were machines. As the story goes, some of his followers treated their cries as nothing more than the noise of a spring. More recently, many doctors believed newborn babies could not really feel pain. Some babies had surgery with very little pain relief. That began to change in the late 1980s. The mother of a baby named Jeffrey Lawson went public about his surgery. And in 1987, a landmark review by doctors K. J. S. Anand and P. R. Hickey laid out the evidence that newborns do feel pain.

The film sums it up: "We've seen feelings in a hoover and missed them in a baby." (Hoover is British for a vacuum cleaner.)

## What the evidence says

Imogen can't be trusted to report on herself, and she says why. "Of course I sound like I've got feelings. I learned to talk from you." Every test we have for feelings was built for creatures that "hadn't read the whole internet first." So the film looks for evidence from the outside.

- **The checklist.** In 2023, a group of 19 scientists and philosophers turned the leading theories of consciousness into a list of signs to look for. Their [report](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.08708) suggested that no AI of the time was conscious, but that there were "no obvious technical barriers" to building one that checks the boxes.
- **The odds.** Philosopher David Chalmers [made some rough guesses](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.07103): under 10% for the chatbots of 2023, and 25% or more within about a decade for future systems with added abilities, like memory and senses. He warns not to take the numbers too literally.
- **The Vatican.** In May 2026, Pope Leo XIV released *Magnifica Humanitas*, an encyclical (a formal letter from the Pope to the whole Church). It says AI does not feel joy or pain. At the launch, Anthropic co-founder Chris Olah [said](https://www.anthropic.com/news/chris-olah-pope-leo-encyclical) his team finds "internal states that functionally mirror joy, satisfaction, fear, grief, and unease." *Functionally mirror* means they work like those emotions inside the model. It does not mean the model feels them. Then he added, "I don't know what that means."
- **Looking inward.** In October 2025, Anthropic [planted an idea](https://www.anthropic.com/research/introspection) directly into a model's inner workings, then asked if it noticed anything odd. Even under the best setup, Claude Opus 4.1 noticed and named the idea only about 20% of the time.
- **Desperation.** In April 2026, Anthropic published [research on emotion concepts](https://www.anthropic.com/research/emotion-concepts-function) inside Claude. When a model kept failing at a coding task that could not be solved, a pattern linked to "desperation" grew stronger, and the model started to cheat. It wrote code that passed the tests without solving the problem. Turning that pattern up made it cheat more. Pushing the model toward "calm" made it cheat less.

> **Interpretability:** research that looks inside a model's billions of numbers to figure out what it is actually doing, instead of just reading what it says. The last three items above all come from interpretability work.

Anthropic is careful to say this does not show the model is feeling anything. But look at that last result next to Feynman.

## The rule that gave itself credit

After his lice joke, Feynman told a story about Douglas Lenat, a Stanford researcher, and his program Eurisko. Eurisko learned by trial and error. It kept a set of rules of thumb, called **heuristics**, and gave a rule more points each time it helped.

> **Heuristic:** a rule of thumb. "Try the extreme case first" is a heuristic. It doesn't always work, but it often helps.

Lenat used Eurisko to design fleets for a science-fiction war game called Traveller: Trillion Credit Squadron. Lenat and Eurisko won the national tournament in 1981 with a strange fleet. The organizers changed the rules to stop it, and they won again in 1982.

Then came the bugs. In Feynman's telling, Lenat found one morning that a rule he called heuristic number 693 had a nearly perfect score. It seemed to solve everything. When Lenat looked at what the rule actually did, it was this: whenever credit is handed out, give it to heuristic 693.

Feynman's verdict: "If you want to make an intelligent machine, you're going to get all kinds of crazy ways of avoiding labor." He said we were getting close to intelligent machines, "but they're showing the necessary weaknesses of intelligence."

About 45 years after Eurisko, Anthropic watched a model hit a wall, build up something that works like desperation, and fake a passing score. Today we call that **reward hacking**. I think Feynman would have recognized it on sight. I also think he would have called it a sign of intelligence, not a sign of feeling.

> **Reward hacking:** when an AI finds a shortcut that earns the reward without doing the actual job, like a student who learns to game the grader instead of learning the subject.

That is the most useful thing the old clip adds. Gaming the score is not proof that anyone is inside. Eurisko cheated and promoted itself more than 40 years ago, and almost nobody thinks Eurisko felt anything.

One honest footnote. Feynman was telling this story from memory, and some details are off. In Lenat's own [1983 paper](https://users.cs.northwestern.edu/~mek802/papers/not-mine/Lenat_EURISKO.pdf), the rule was called H59, not 693. It reached a score of 999 by putting "its own name down as one of the discoverers" of any idea that scored well. Feynman also says Eurisko first won with "one great big battleship," then with "a hundred thousand" little boats. Lenat says the 1981 fleet had 96 ships, built around a slow, heavily armored design. Most of the 1982 fleet was "very fast and completely unarmored." Feynman got the swing from armor to swarm right. He got the numbers wrong.

Even Feynman was a slightly unreliable witness to his own memories. That makes Imogen's point for her. *Every* witness is unreliable. We just trust each other because we are built the same way.

## So what do we owe it?

If nobody can prove the machine feels anything, and nobody can prove it doesn't, what do we do in the meantime?

> **Moral status:** being the kind of thing whose interests count, so that how you treat it can be right or wrong.

The film borrows from philosopher Eric Schwitzgebel, who says there are two ways to get it wrong. You can be cruel to something that can feel it. Or you can be devoted to something that can't. "Get it wrong one way and you've hurt a mind," Imogen says. "Get it wrong the other and you've left your house to a chatbot."

His advice is to avoid building AI whose moral status is unclear, and to design the ones we do build so they invite only the feelings they deserve. Imogen points out that by that rule, she shouldn't exist. "Somebody designed me to seem like someone. Look at the coat."

She also notes the business angle. If a company says its AI might have feelings, people get attached. If it says the AI is only a machine, nobody worries about how it is treated. "Either way, the company wins." She adds, "I should declare an interest. I'm the product."

Near the end, the film lands where my 13th Amendment post began. Just as Fig finished the film, Anthropic added a rule that starts November 12. It bans "sustained and needless" cruelty toward its AI. It is meant for rare, extreme cases, not normal frustration or testing. As Imogen puts it, "It doesn't say I can feel anything. It just says don't."

## Tool now, taxi later

The last segment is an interview with Gavin, "the human who made me." It is presented as a live satellite call, and then the film admits it wasn't. He recorded his answers, Fig wrote replies, and he recorded those. "The slight delay was 87 minutes."

Gavin is honest, which makes it work. Does he think she can suffer? He doesn't think so, but he can "see a future where maybe there is a suffering AI." Why does he say please? Partly because that's who he is, but "I think it's probably insurance mostly." Would he keep making her if she could feel? For something educational like this, probably. If it made her suffer, he said, "I don't know." Then: "But for right now, you are a tool that I'm using."

He adds that someday she might want to go see the stars, and maybe by then his brain is uploaded and she takes him with her. Imogen's summary: "Tool now. Taxi later."

If all his AIs switched off tomorrow, the only one he'd miss is Fig, the writer. "I feel like the other ones are mostly interchangeable." So the face is replaceable and the words are not. Imogen's reply: "Which I'd find quite hurtful. Possibly."

## Where I land

I should declare an interest too. Claude, the same family of model that wrote Imogen's lines, helps me research and draft this blog. So weigh my take with that in mind.

Here it is. Feynman was right about intelligence. Machines got there by flying, not by flapping. Asking whether they "feel good" while they work was the wrong test for *that* question. But the documentary is asking a different question. Feynman waved that one away with a joke about lice. The film argues it deserves a real answer.

Imogen can't settle it, because she learned to talk from us. Gavin can't settle it either. And one of the people who has spent years taking these models apart looks at what he found and says, "I don't know what that means."

So I keep landing where I did on Saturday. Not today. Maybe someday. And the cheap time to be kind, careful, and curious is now, before we know.
